# 02 · Context pack

The map tells you how work moves. The context pack holds the facts every stage assumes but nobody has written down. Together they make up the foundation layer.

Each of these is a plain markdown file, one topic per file, kept in the shared drive your team already uses. Every tool and every person reads from them. When a fact changes, you correct it in one place and everything downstream gets the update.

---

## Why this exists

In most owner-led businesses, each tool and each person gets briefed separately, by the owner, from memory. The quoting spreadsheet doesn't know what the estimator decided last week. The proposal doesn't know what the site visit found. The AI chat you used on Tuesday starts from zero on Wednesday. None of that is because the tools are weak. It's because there is no shared floor for them to stand on. The context pack is that floor, and it ends the re-briefing.

It's also what makes a new hire productive in week one instead of month three. Most of what someone asks in their first month is a question one of these files already answers.

---

## The files

**`what-we-take-on.md`** covers the work you'll accept and the work you turn away. Job types, customer types, service area, size range, minimum order or project size, lead times you can commit to, terms you'll accept and terms you won't. Write the disqualifiers out explicitly. The things that kill a request on sight are more useful than the things that qualify it, and they're faster to apply.

**`standing-assumptions.md`** lists every default your numbers rest on. Pricing rules, labor rates, markups, target margins, minimums, standard lead times, payment terms, contingencies. For each one, note whether it's a firm standard or something decided per job. This file is also the first place to look when a quote or an AI output looks wrong.

**`customer-and-market-beliefs.md`** has one short section per customer segment or market you serve. Why you're there, what you believe about it, and what would change your mind. Date each section. This is what lets recurring reports, proposals, and marketing say something specific instead of repeating generic industry talk.

**`glossary.md`** is your shorthand. Customer nicknames, job codes, abbreviations, internal terms, and what you mean by words like *hot*, *on hold*, or *dead*. It's a small file with outsized value. It's what lets an AI or a new hire read an internal email without having to ask.

**`filing-conventions.md`** writes out your folder structure. The hierarchy, the naming pattern, what goes where at each stage, and what gets archived when. You probably already have a good structure in your head and in practice. This makes it enforceable by something other than you.

**`definitions-of-done.md`** says, for each thing you produce (a quote, a proposal, a job ticket, a drawing, an invoice, a status report), what has to be true before it's finished. Make it specific and checkable. "Every number traces to a named source" is a definition of done. "Thorough" is not.

**`approval-gates.md`** covers what leaves the business, who signs it, and what they're actually checking when they sign. This is the file your partners, your insurer, and your lawyer will care about. It keeps a human in the chain of responsibility rather than just on the org chart.

---

## How to build them

Don't sit down to write these. They fall out of the mapping interview. Every time the owner says a number, a threshold, or a rule during session two, it belongs in one of these files. At the end of each mapping session, have the AI pull those out and draft the files. Then the owner confirms them.

Two rules apply.

**Mark inferred facts.** Use the same convention as the map: `[UNCONFIRMED]` on anything that wasn't said out loud. Nobody should ever wonder whether a threshold came from the owner or from a model's guess.

**Date every file, and re-confirm quarterly.** A pricing rule from eighteen months ago that nobody has revisited is worse than no rule, because it gets applied with confidence. Put `Last confirmed: YYYY-MM-DD` at the top of each file and treat a stale date as a flag.

---

## The test

Hand the context pack to someone who doesn't work in the business and ask them to sort five real incoming requests. If they can tell you which ones you'd take on, the pack is doing its job. They don't yet need to know how you'd price them.
